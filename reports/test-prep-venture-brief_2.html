Bottom line

01

What your rules eliminate

You've ruled out the tests whose owners lock content and enforce trademark against prep — the admissions/language oligopoly. That's the right call: they don't license questions, they litigate over paraphrased ones, they block you from even naming the product cleanly, and they bundle free official prep to crush third parties. What's left are tests where either the content is effectively public, or original blueprint-based practice is a normal, tolerated market.

Out of scope EXCLUDED

  • SAT / ACT / AP — College Board, no commercial license, active litigation
  • IELTS / TOEFL / PTE / Duolingo — trademark-heavy, official prep given away free
  • GRE / GMAT / LSAT / MCAT — ETS / GMAC / LSAC / AAMC, same posture

Survives the filter ANALYZED

Public content · or original prep operates openly
  • IT & cloud certifications — public blueprints, originals legal
  • Pre-employment aptitude & psychometric — style-based, originals legal
  • Academic olympiads — problems effectively public domain
  • Government / competitive exams — past papers public
  • Finance & business designations — third parties operate (with caution)
  • Professional licensing — third parties operate (with caution)
02

How "AI builds the banks" changes the math

Building the question bank with AI is the right instinct, but it cuts two ways, and both matter for which category you pick.

The upside: it collapses the single biggest cost and moat in this industry. Incumbents like UWorld and Kaplan win partly on armies of human item-writers producing calibrated questions with rich explanations. AI turns that from a multi-year content operation into a pipeline — and, critically, it makes staying current cheap, which is decisive in fast-churning categories like cloud certs where blueprints get rewritten yearly.

The catch: if it's cheap for you, it's cheap for everyone. Content stops being defensible. Whatever moat you build has to live somewhere else — verified accuracy, difficulty calibration from real user data, the fidelity of the test-day simulation, community, and distribution.

The accuracy problem you must design around AI-generated multiple-choice items routinely ship subtle defects: two defensible "correct" answers, a wrong answer key, mis-calibrated difficulty, or a factual error. So the pipeline isn't just generate — it's generate → validate → calibrate live. That validation loop, not the raw generation, is the actual asset.

This single point reorders the categories. Where answers are objectively verifiable — numerical/logical reasoning, coding — you can auto-check correctness at scale, which neutralizes AI's biggest weakness. Where answers are factual and fast-moving (cloud certs), you need a currency-and-QA layer. Where they're high-stakes and safety-critical (medical licensing), an AI-made wrong answer is a liability, and you need expert review that erases the cost advantage. That gradient is exactly why aptitude scores so well below, and why medical licensing scores poorly.

05

Whitespace audit — why C2 and C3 look empty

The sharp diligence question: if the demand is obvious and the supply is thin, why? That's the line between a gap and a graveyard. For C2 and C3 the answers point in opposite directions — and that difference reorders them.

Revised verdict

C2's whitespace is real but capped by a structural reason. C3's whitespace mostly isn't there — it looks open because the content is free, which is exactly why it's not a business. Lead with C2; keep C3 optional.

  • →The engine is shared: auto-verifiable reasoning practice powers both job-aptitude and competition/coding. So point the go-to-market at the surface with real volume and search demand — C2 — and keep a competition mode as a low-priority expansion, not a co-bet.
C2 · APTITUDE

The gap is real — but capped by LTV

LEAD WITH THIS

Demand is exactly the shape you want: people search for the precise test they're about to sit — "Amazon online assessment," "SHL numerical," "McKinsey Solve" — and the supply is a few dated, paywalled incumbents plus a swamp of low-quality free bait. That long tail is genuinely underserved by anything good, and AI-cheap content is the unlock. This is the best fit in the whole list for "flooded search, nothing quality to serve."

Why no great one existsLTV is the killer. A candidate preps intensively for a few weeks in one job hunt, then churns forever — no habit, no recurring need. Even the 30-year incumbent (JobTestPrep, 1M+ users) monetizes one-time packs, not subscriptions. A better product doesn't unlock proportionally more money, so incumbents settled for SEO cash businesses and VCs stayed away.
Moving targetEmployers are shifting to gamified / AI-scored formats partly to defeat coaching — but prep already follows, and the formats still measure the same constructs. You're in a permanent cosmetic arms race, not a losing one.
The real playA modern, AI-native, SEO-first product can take share with better margins and a great free tier — if you design around the ceiling from day one (broaden the job-to-be-done, or go geography-first, or flip B2B to the assessment side).

OPTIONAL · ENGINE-SHARE

06

Solving the aptitude "too many formats" problem

Your concern is real: SHL vs Korn Ferry vs Cubiks, numerical vs verbal vs logical vs diagrammatic vs situational-judgment, adaptive vs linear, plus every employer's own wrapper. It looks like an unbounded surface. It isn't — the sprawl is entirely cosmetic. Underneath, the number of things actually being measured is small and finite. The fix is a two-layer architecture: a shared construct engine, with lightweight test-profiles skinned on top.

Layer 2 · the surface you sell

Test profiles — each one is a config file, not a content pipeline

A "profile" maps a target (a publisher style or a named employer's assessment) to a configuration over the shared engine: which constructs, how many items, time limits, adaptive or linear, answer format, cosmetic styling. Adding a new "test" takes hours, not a new bank.

Layer 1 · the engine (your real asset)

A finite set of measured constructs

AI generates parameterized items per construct, tagged by difficulty. One numerical-reasoning generator serves SHL, Korn Ferry, Cubiks and any employer at once. Correctness is auto-verifiable for the core constructs — which is what makes AI generation trustworthy here.

Numerical reasoning Verbal reasoning Logical / inductive Diagrammatic / deductive Abstract / matrices Situational judgment* Error-checking* Mechanical / spatial* Personality — coach, don't drill*
How to actually sequence it Ship three constructs first — numerical, verbal, logical cover the vast majority of graduate and professional screening — wrapped in the two or three highest-traffic profiles. Expand profiles as demand shows up. The starred constructs are different beasts: SJTs need scenario libraries with calibrated best/worst keying, and personality tests aren't "practiceable" (you coach strategy, not answers), so scope them as later or optional.

And here's the payoff: once the engine exists, the fragmentation you feared flips into a moat. A competitor hand-authoring content can't cheaply keep up as employers switch vendors and invent new formats. Your config-driven system spins up a new profile in an afternoon. The same pattern generalizes — an IT-cert build is a domain-objective engine + per-exam blueprint profiles; a government-exam build is a topic engine + per-exam pattern profiles. Build the engine once, skin it many times.

07

The free-tools-first path

Your sequencing — free tools to build an audience, monetize a substantial market later — works cleanly in exactly the two front-runner categories, because both have high organic search demand and an obvious paid upgrade. It works poorly in finance and licensing, where buyers skip straight to paid products. That alignment is itself a reason to favour C1 and C2.

01

Free, ungated practice acquisition

Unlimited AI-generated questions per construct or exam objective, no signup. Ranks on the searches people already make ("free AWS practice questions", "SHL numerical practice"). This is the top of the funnel and the SEO engine.

02

Account for progress retention

Save history, track weak areas, get a readiness estimate. Free, but now you have a returning user and the item-response data that calibrates your bank — the flywheel that makes questions better than a generic chatbot's.

03

Paid depth monetization

Full-length timed mocks under real test-day conditions, employer- or vendor-specific packs, richer explanations, and analytics. This is where the substantial market gets monetized — after the free tools have already won the audience.

The defensibility answer, concretely Since AI-made questions aren't a moat, the paid tier has to sell what a chatbot can't hand someone: a faithful test-day simulation, difficulty calibrated on real outcomes, verified accuracy, and eventually a score users trust to predict the real thing. That trust — "practice here, and your real score lands where we said" — is the durable asset. Everything upstream is acquisition.

Q1

How to beat the LTV ceiling

The one question that decides whether C2 is a business or just a product. Three routes: broaden into career-readiness, go geography-first on grad funnels, or flip B2B / white-label to the assessment side. Worked through in the deep-dive.

Q2

Which single test / construct to ship first?

One AWS or CompTIA exam? Or numerical + verbal + logical for the graduate-hiring funnel? Pick the narrowest thing that proves the wedge.

Q3

Start-market vs global upside

Both front-runners are global by nature — decide whether to seed in one region (Gulf / India diaspora) for distribution, while keeping the product globally usable.

Q4

The QA / calibration pipeline

How you validate AI items (auto-solve, uniqueness checks, difficulty estimation, live calibration). This loop is the real asset — worth designing early.

Q5

Distribution & SEO

Free tools only work if found. What's the content/search strategy that gets the free tier ranking before competitors notice?

Q6

What "trusted score" requires

To sell prediction, you need a concordance between your practice scores and real outcomes. How do you collect that signal, and by when?
